# Phase 7A Story Form test — SIT164 under all five forms

**Situation:** SIT164 — "A younger child wants to join the game"
**storySeed:** Kavi and friends are playing a game when a younger child asks if they can join. Want: keep the game moving the way it was planned. Obstacle: the younger child may not know the rules or play at the same level. Tension: protect the fun for the group without making the younger child feel unwanted.
**Need:** NEED_COMPASSION (Friendship & Peer Dynamics)

Same situation, same want/obstacle/tension seed, run through all five forms with no cross-contamination — each plan only uses the mechanism that form allows itself.

---

## F01 — The Journey of Trying
*Question: Can I find a way?*

1. Kavi and friends are deep in their favourite game — finally working well — when a younger child asks to join.
2. **Attempt 1:** Kavi lets the child play by the normal rules. It falls apart immediately — the child can't keep up, the game stalls.
3. **Consequence:** the older kids get frustrated; the fun Kavi was protecting is now gone anyway.
4. **Attempt 2 (different action, not a repeat):** Kavi tries slowing the pace down for everyone. Better, but now the older friends are visibly bored.
5. **Adjustment:** Kavi notices the problem isn't speed — it's that there's only one way to play.
6. **Attempt 3:** Kavi invents a small side-role in the game that fits the younger child's level without changing the main game for everyone else.
7. It works — not perfectly, but well enough that everyone, including the younger child, is playing something real.
8. **Turning point realized:** "I don't have to make one version of the game work for everyone — I just have to find the version that does."

**Ending:** Kavi keeps adjusting the game slightly every time someone new wants in — it's just what Kavi does now.

---

## F02 — The Discovery Journey
*Question: What is this / what will I discover?*

1. Kavi notices the younger child isn't just standing there to interrupt — they're watching the game intently, mouthing the rules under their breath.
2. Curious, Kavi asks what they're doing. The child says they've been playing a version of this game at home — with a twist Kavi's never heard of.
3. Kavi tries the twist, half-expecting it to ruin things.
4. It doesn't — it changes the game in a way that's actually more interesting.
5. The friends notice and want to know where the twist came from; the younger child, for the first time, is the one explaining the rules instead of learning them.
6. Kavi discovers something unexpected: the "interruption" brought something into the game that was never there before.
7. **Final discovery reframes the opening:** the game Kavi was protecting wasn't finished — it just didn't know it yet.

**Ending:** circular/reflective — Kavi looks at the game now being played and can't quite picture the old version anymore.

---

## F03 — The Shift in Seeing
*Question: What if I am seeing this differently?*

1. **Establish belief:** Kavi is sure that a younger kid joining always means the game gets ruined — it's happened before.
2. The child asks to join. Kavi's first instinct (unspoken) is already "this is going to fall apart."
3. **Evidence that seems to confirm it:** the child does fumble the first round, exactly as Kavi expected.
4. **Something doesn't fit:** but the child isn't upset or clingy about it — they laugh it off and ask a sharp question about the rules that even Kavi hadn't thought about.
5. Kavi starts to notice the child isn't "a younger kid who'll ruin it" — they're just a beginner, the way Kavi once was.
6. **Revealing moment:** Kavi remembers being the one nobody wanted to let play, once.
7. Kavi makes a different choice — not out of duty, but because the belief itself has quietly changed.

**Ending:** Kavi waves the child back in for the next round without making it a moment — the shift shows in the action, not a speech.

---

## F04 — The Connection Journey
*Question: Can I understand/connect with this person?*

1. Kavi and friends are halfway through the game when the younger child appears beside them, asking to join.
2. Kavi's first response is the easy, automatic one — a soft "maybe later" — based on assuming the child just wants to be included at any cost.
3. Something reveals that's not quite right: the child doesn't leave, but also doesn't push — they just keep watching one particular part of the game closely.
4. Kavi looks again and notices what the child is actually drawn to — not the whole game, just one piece of it.
5. Kavi asks, directly, what the child wants to play. The answer isn't what Kavi assumed.
6. Kavi changes their response — not "let them play" as an obligation, but inviting them into the specific part they were watching.

**Ending:** shown, not stated — Kavi shifts over and makes literal space in the circle. The connection is the resolution.

---

## F05 — The Unexpected Turn
*Question: What do I do when things don't go as planned?*

1. Kavi has today's game planned out exactly — who's playing what, how long, how it ends.
2. The younger child's request to join is the first disruption to that plan.
3. Kavi tries to fold the child in without changing anything about the plan — it doesn't work; the plan starts slipping.
4. **Second disruption:** one of Kavi's friends has to leave early, throwing the original plan off even further.
5. Kavi tries to patch the original plan back together around both changes. It keeps not fitting.
6. Kavi gives up trying to restore the original version of the afternoon.
7. **Adaptation:** Kavi lets the game reshape itself around who's actually there and what they actually want to do.
8. What emerges isn't the game Kavi planned — it's a different, looser afternoon that turns out to be better than the plan was.

**Ending:** Kavi stops mentally comparing it to the plan at all.

---

## Verdict — do these feel like five different stories?

Yes, and importantly for structurally sound reasons, not just surface variation:

- **F01** is the only one with real attempt/consequence/attempt escalation — a skill problem solved by iteration.
- **F02** is the only one where the younger child is a source of new information, not a problem — Kavi ends up *learning something the child knew*.
- **F03** is the only one that runs entirely on Kavi's internal belief changing — the external events (child fumbles, laughs it off) are almost incidental; a plot summary of just the actions would undersell the whole story.
- **F04** is the only one where the resolution is a specific, small, physical gesture ("moved over, made space") rather than a decision or an idea.
- **F05** is the only one where the younger child isn't even the main disruption by the end — it becomes about Kavi's whole afternoon plan falling apart, which is a materially different story than the other four.

Two of the five (F02 and F04) both end with something like "the game changes to include the child," but they get there by different engines — F02 via curiosity/discovery of a new rule, F04 via noticing what the child specifically wants — and they'd read as different stories on the page, not palette-swapped versions of each other. F01, F03, and F05 aren't close to each other or to F02/F04 at all.

One genuine risk to flag: **F02 and F04 are the closest pair** and would need real editorial discipline in the templates/writing layer to stay distinct across many situations, not just this one — SIT164 has a natural "the newcomer brings something of value" angle that makes both forms pull toward a similar warm ending. Worth stress-testing that pair specifically on a situation where the "connection" and "discovery" angles are less naturally aligned.
