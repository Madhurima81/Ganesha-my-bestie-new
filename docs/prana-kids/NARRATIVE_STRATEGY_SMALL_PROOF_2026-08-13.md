# Narrative Strategy Small Proof

Date: 2026-08-13
Scope: Small proof-of-concept before any engine coding. One emotional spine, one situation lane, three narrative strategies, three different story shapes.

## Goal

Test the core claim:

The real variation engine should be the **narrative strategy**, not the emotional spine alone and not the old overloaded template layer.

This proof keeps the emotional meaning constant and changes only the storytelling strategy.

## Fixed Elements

Situation lane:

- based on the real library theme of comparison / identity pressure
- closest live situation reference: `SIT077` "Why can't you be more like [cousin/friend]?"

Emotional spine:

- false belief: `I have to be like someone else to be valued.`
- true belief: `My unique strengths make me valuable.`

Character:

- child-facing protagonist, lightly inspired by the identity/comparison lane

Form gate:

- `Transformation / Perspective Shift`

This means the emotional truth stays the same in all three examples.
Only the narrative strategy changes.

## Strategy 1: Conversation

Core sequence:

`hurt -> question -> reflective response -> deeper question -> recognition -> changed action`

### Story

Mina held her drawing against Rhea's and felt her shoulders drop. Rhea's picture looked neat and bright. Mina's had smudges, heavy lines, and a sun that leaned to one side.

"Why is yours better?" Mina asked.

Grandfather Ganesha, who was sitting nearby with his basket of crayons, did not answer right away. He asked, "Better for what?"

Mina frowned. "Better for the teacher. Better like the example. Better like... everybody else's."

Ganesha nodded and looked at Mina's page. "What do you see in yours that is not in theirs?"

Mina almost said, "Nothing." But then she stopped. In her picture, the wind looked real. The tree branches bent as if they were moving. The grass looked wild, not stiff.

"It looks like it's doing something," Mina said slowly.

"Yes," said Ganesha. "Rhea drew the shape of the tree. You drew what the day felt like."

Mina looked again. For the first time, she was not measuring the two pictures by sameness. She picked up her green crayon and added one more line of grass, this one even wilder than before.

### Why it feels different

- variation comes from dialogue
- the shift happens through questioning
- the story feels relational and reflective

## Strategy 2: Noticing Sequence

Core sequence:

`painful comparison -> notice 1 -> notice 2 -> notice 3 -> connection -> recognition`

### Story

Mina stared at her drawing and then at Rhea's.

Rhea's lines were straight. Mina's were not.

Rhea's flower petals matched each other. Mina's petals opened in different directions.

Mina pressed her lips together. She was about to fold her paper in half when she noticed the basil plant by the window.

Its leaves were all different sizes.

Then she looked outside. A row of clouds stretched across the sky, but none of them had the same shape.

Then she looked down at the tray of shells on the classroom shelf. Some were striped. Some were chipped. Some curled tightly. None looked copied from the others.

Mina slowly looked back at her drawing.

Her flower did not look like Rhea's. But it did look alive. The petals seemed to be opening in their own way, the same way the basil leaves and clouds and shells each did.

Mina set her folded paper flat again. She did not erase the uneven lines. She darkened them.

### Why it feels different

- variation comes from accumulation of observations
- no guide has to explain the meaning
- the shift feels quiet and perceptual

## Strategy 3: Cause-Effect

Core sequence:

`comparison pain -> copy attempt -> worse consequence -> own attempt -> better consequence -> recognition`

### Story

When Mina saw Rhea's drawing, she decided exactly what to do.

She would make hers match.

She erased her thick tree trunk and redrew it thinner. She rubbed out the swirling sky and replaced it with careful little blue lines. She even changed the crooked bird in the corner into one that looked more like Rhea's.

When she finished, Mina sat back and waited to feel better.

But the feeling did not come.

The drawing looked cleaner now, but it also felt farther away. It did not feel like the windy afternoon she had been trying to remember. It felt like someone else's page wearing her name.

Mina looked at the patch of erased paper, then at the one part she had forgotten to change: the corner where her first wild line of grass was still showing.

She took her dark green crayon and drew the grass back in. Then the bent branches. Then the uneven sun. Little by little, the page stopped looking correct and started looking true.

By the time she finished, Mina understood something she had not been able to hear while comparing. A drawing did not become good by becoming someone else's. It became good by becoming fully itself.

### Why it feels different

- variation comes from consequences, not conversation or noticing
- the shift is learned through action
- the story has a stronger before/after contrast

## What This Proof Shows

All three stories keep the same core emotional truth:

- same false belief
- same true belief
- same comparison pressure
- same general form lane

But they feel different because the **narrative strategy** changes:

- `Conversation` feels guided and reflective
- `Noticing Sequence` feels quiet and observational
- `Cause-Effect` feels experiential and consequence-driven

This is stronger evidence for variation than simply renaming templates or lightly reshuffling beats.

## What This Proof Does Not Yet Prove

This proof is intentionally small.

It does not yet prove:

- that all eight strategies are equally strong
- that every strategy works for every emotional spine
- that library-driven content selection is already solved
- that prose generation can do this reliably at runtime

It only proves the most important first question:

Can one emotional spine produce genuinely different stories if strategy, rather than template, carries the variation?

Current answer:

`Yes, plausibly enough to justify a second proof.`
